A few years ago, I found a cassette tape in my grandmother's shoebox. "Christmas 1974," it said, in her handwriting. Nobody could play it — not our laptops, not our phones, not anything we owned.

We eventually tracked down an old deck at a thrift store. Out came her laugh. My dad as a teenager. The sound of dishes clattering in the background. Twenty-three minutes. We nearly lost it because we waited too long to check.

That experience rewired how I think about preservation. The digital stuff we're making now is more fragile than we admit. Hard drives fail in a few years. SSDs lose data if left unpowered. Platforms shut down. But the real enemy isn't hardware — it's that "I'll sort it later" folder that just keeps growing. Preservation is a habit problem, not a tech problem.

I wrote down everything I wish I'd known sooner: the three-copy rule that archivists swear by, which file formats will actually outlast us, and the thing people regret most — never recording a loved one's voice. Photos show what someone looked like. Voices show who they were. The microphone in your pocket right now is better than anything available to a professional photographer a generation ago. You don't need the perfect moment. It won't arrive. You just hit record.
