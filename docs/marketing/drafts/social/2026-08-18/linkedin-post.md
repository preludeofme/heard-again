# LinkedIn Post — "How to Record Your Grandparents' Voices Before the Stories Go Quiet"

---

What's a memory you can replay perfectly right now — and have no recording of?

I sat down with my grandfather a few years ago and asked how he met my grandmother. He was 91. The story lasted forty minutes and wandered in six directions. I meant to press record. I told myself I'd remember the details. I still remember his hands moving while he talked, but the words themselves have started to blur.

Here's the imbalance that surprises people: we have more photographs of our grandparents than any generation before us, and almost no recordings of their voice. A photograph freezes a face. Only a recording keeps the sound of someone — the cadence, the laugh they saved for certain stories, the way they paused before saying something that mattered.

The most common regret I hear from people who've lost a grandparent isn't that they didn't love them enough. It's that they never recorded a conversation. Always some reason to wait. The window doesn't announce when it's closing, and the next ordinary afternoon is the perfect time.

So here's the practical part I've learned: don't interview. Name one specific memory instead. "Could I record this so I don't forget the details?" is care, not formality. Keep it to one question at a time and let the tangents run — that's where the real texture lives. Then keep more than one copy, and name the file so a stranger could understand it. The context you write down now is gold to someone who never met the person behind the voice.

At Heard Again, we build tools for this — always with consent at the center and the family in control of what gets kept. But the most important step happens before any tool. It's deciding that a voice you love is worth keeping. Everything else can follow from there.
